At hand

The agents you
already have at hand.

An AI agent is an AI that doesn't just answer. You give it a goal, it works through the steps on its own and hands you finished work. In November 2026, you don't have to build anything to get one, it already lives in mainstream tools. Here are the four families I recommend, with one example to try for each.

01 · On your files

The Claude app with a folder

In the Claude desktop app, you give Claude access to a folder on your computer. It reads your files, organizes them and creates new ones. This mode used to be called Cowork. In late 2026, it's merging into the normal conversation, rolling out on the Pro, Max and Ultra plans. You ask, and Claude decides on its own whether to answer or to get to work.

During a task, it can use your connected apps (Google Drive, Gmail, Microsoft 365, Slack), browse the web with its built-in browser or with Claude in Chrome, and repeat a task on a schedule, every Monday for example.

Try this. Put this month's invoices in a folder, open it to Claude and write "Read every PDF invoice in this folder and make me a table with the date, the supplier, the amount before tax, the VAT and the total, plus a total row." You get a spreadsheet ready to open.

What you need. A paid plan, Claude Pro at $20 a month before tax or higher.

02 · A finished deliverable

ChatGPT Work

The ChatGPT desktop app, on Mac and Windows, has two modes, Chat and Work. Work is an agent for longer tasks with several steps. It hands you a document, a spreadsheet, a presentation, a report or even a site. In the desktop app, it can use your files and your apps, always with your permission.

Try this. Put three quotes in a folder, switch to Work and write "Compare these three quotes in a table with the prices, lead times, warranties and what's included. Tell me which one you'd pick for [your criterion] and why, in 5 lines." No more reading three PDFs side by side, and the decision stays yours.

What you need. A paid plan, ChatGPT Plus costs $20 a month.

03 · On the web

AI browsers

An AI browser sees the page you're reading and can act on it when you ask. No more copy-pasting a page into a chat. The best known is Comet, from Perplexity. Atlas, OpenAI's browser, closed on August 9, 2026, and its features moved into the ChatGPT desktop app. The Claude app also has its own built-in browser, and the Claude in Chrome extension puts Claude to work inside your Chrome.

Try this. On a product page, with two other shops open in tabs, you ask "Compare with the two other tabs and tell me which one is cheapest with delivery before Saturday." It reads the three pages and answers.

My rule. It can fill the cart, but you're the one who checks it and pays.

04 · On its own, every day

No-code automation tools

Zapier, Make, n8n or Lindy connect your apps to each other. Definition

What is an
AI agent?

In one sentence

An AI agent is a large language model that's been given tools, an execution loop and a goal. Simon Willison, one of the clearest voices on the topic, sums it up with the formula LLM + tools + loop + goal. That's it. No magic, just four building blocks working together.

In practice, an AI agent starts from an intention you give it in plain English (or French, or any language an LLM understands). Instead of just replying with text like a classic chatbot, it decides to call tools. Read a file, open a web page, fill in a spreadsheet, draft an email. It looks at the result, thinks, tries again if needed. The loop stops when the goal is reached, or when a human takes back control.

Agent vs chatbot · the core difference

A chatbot answers. An agent acts. ChatGPT in Chat mode is a chatbot. You ask a question, it generates text, and you're the one who applies it. Switch to Work mode, or open a folder to the Claude app, and you have an agent. You tell it "prepare a brief for each of the 10 prospects in this spreadsheet" and it actually reads the file, writes the 10 briefs, puts them in one document and hands it back. That ability to do the work, not just talk about it, changes everything.

Workflow or agent · who decides the steps?

A workflow follows steps you set in advance. "When an email arrives with an invoice, save the attachment in this folder and add a row to the spreadsheet." That's what a classic Zapier or Make automation does. It's reliable, predictable, and it never leaves the planned path. An agent picks its own steps. You give it a goal, "tidy up this folder and give me a summary of unpaid invoices," and it decides what to read, in what order, and when it's done.

My rule is simple. If you can write the steps down in advance, a workflow is enough and costs less. If the task changes every time, you need an agent. Many tools mix the two, like Zapier's agents or the Claude app's scheduled tasks.

Why 2026 is the year of agents

In 2023, people mostly talked about "AI assistants" that suggested text. In 2024, the first real agent frameworks came out (LangChain, AutoGen). In 2025, models' reasoning crossed a threshold that made agents reliable on complex tasks. 2026 is the year AI agents became usable day to day, even for non-developers.

Three things changed at once. Models got better at reasoning. Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon, released between late September and mid-October 2026, reason over several steps without losing the thread. The MCP standard took hold. Since late 2024, an agent can plug into any service through the Model Context Protocol. And agents showed up where you already work, in the Claude app, in ChatGPT, in your browser and in the automation tools you may have used for years.

The result is that what took a team of developers and two months of work in 2023 now gets done in an afternoon in 2026. That's why I wrote this page. The barrier to entry has collapsed, and a lot of people don't know it yet.

The quote to remember

"An agent is an LLM that runs a loop of tools in an environment to achieve a goal," as Simon Willison puts it. He created Datasette and has been a go-to observer of LLMs since 2022.

The five steps I follow every time

  • 1 · Pick a simple, repetitive task. Just one. The one that bugs you every week and always follows the same pattern.
  • 2 · Pick the agent that fits, using the table right above.
  • 3 · Give it only what it needs. A test folder rather than your whole drive, one connected app rather than ten.
  • 4 · Test while staying in control. In the Claude app, a setting decides how much it works on its own. Auto, the default on Pro, lets it move ahead with automatic checks. Manual makes it ask for your approval before every action. For your first tasks, switch to Manual. In ChatGPT Work, nothing touches your files without your permission.
  • 5 · Let it run, with guardrails. Once it has got it right several times in a row, let it move ahead on its own, schedule the task if it comes back, and keep your sign-off for anything that can't be undone.

The hidden costs people forget

What shows up on the bill is never the real cost of an agent. Here are the ones I see coming back all the time.

  • Review · at first, you check everything the agent did. That's normal, and it's what saves you from bad surprises. That time drops as you come to trust it.
  • Usage limits · subscriptions have limits. A big agent task uses far more than a question in the chat. If you hit them often, it's a sign you need the next plan up.
  • Maintenance · an automation that works today can break tomorrow if an app changes. Count a few hours a month to keep your automations in shape.
  • Endless loops · an automation or a scheduled task stuck on an error can burn through your quota or your credit all night. Always set a limit.
  • Learning · the time you spend discovering the tool and refining your instructions. In the first week, count a few hours to find your settings.

When a paid agent is worth it

My simple rule. An agent is worth its price when it saves you at least 3 hours a month. At $20 a month, 3 hours saved at €25 an hour (your baseline personal rate), you're already well ahead. For a sales or contracts agent that saves you 10 hours a month, the return is huge.

What isn't worth it is paying for an agent you barely use. No need to sign up for a $100-a-month tool just to say "I have an AI agent." Start with Claude Pro or ChatGPT Plus at $20 a month, or a free n8n, and move up when the real need shows up.

Under the hood

How an agent is built ·
the technical building blocks.

Whether you go through the Claude app, ChatGPT Work or a no-code tool, an AI agent is always made of the same building blocks. Knowing them helps you pick your tools and understand what's stuck when something breaks. Here are the six core blocks.

Block 1 · The LLM (the brain)

This is the language model that reasons, decides which tool to call and interprets the results. In November 2026, the leaders are Claude (Anthropic), GPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google) and, in Europe, Mistral. The model you pick affects reasoning quality, speed and price. For a personal agent, Claude Sonnet 5.5 is the best trade-off, and you even get it for free. Claude Opus 5.5 is the one Anthropic recommends to start with, very strong on agent tasks. At OpenAI, GPT-6.1 Sol is the right default, and GPT-6 Luna the small, cheap model for simple, repeated tasks. At Google, the new flagship is called Gemini 4 Argon. All models compared · the LLM entry.

Block 2 · Tools (function calling)

Tools are what the agent can call. Read a file, send an email, run a web search, query a service. Technically, it's called function calling. You describe a function to the agent (its name, its parameters, what it does), and it decides when to call it. Without tools, an agent is just a chatbot. Read the Tool use entry.

Block 3 · MCP (Model Context Protocol)

MCP is the open standard Anthropic launched in late 2024, which lets any agent plug into any external service. Instead of coding a custom integration for each tool, you plug in an MCP server and the agent uses it as if it had been built for it. It's the same idea as the Claude app's connected apps, when you plug Gmail or Slack into it. That's what made the agent ecosystem take off in 2025 and 2026. Read the MCP entry.

Block 4 · Memory

Without memory, an agent forgets everything between two tasks. Memory comes in two layers. Short term is the context of the current conversation, up to 1 million tokens with Claude Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5. Long term often relies on embeddings stored in a vector database like Pinecone, Supabase pgvector or Weaviate. Long-term memory lets the agent remember your preferences, past exchanges and documents.

Block 5 · The execution loop (agentic loop)

This is the heart of what separates an agent from a chatbot. The typical loop is think, pick a tool, run it, look at the result, and go again if the goal isn't reached. The loop can run once or a hundred times depending on how complex the task is. Those back-and-forths are what make an agent autonomous. Read the Agentic workflow entry.

Block 6 · Guardrails and human in the loop

Guardrails are the rules that stop the agent from doing something dumb, like touching certain files, going over a budget or sending an email without approval. Human in the loop means asking a human to approve sensitive actions before they run. The Claude app's Manual setting and ChatGPT Work's permissions are exactly that. These two blocks are essential as soon as the agent touches real data.

A simplified diagram of the architecture

Here's how these blocks fit together in practice.

  • You give the agent an intention in plain English.
  • The LLM breaks it into subtasks.
  • For each subtask, the agent picks a tool (read a file, run a search, use a connected app).
  • The tool runs, through MCP or native function calling.
  • The agent looks at the result, keeps it in memory if it's useful, and decides the next step.
  • On sensitive actions, the agent hands control back to you for approval.
  • The loop goes on until the goal is reached, the limit is hit or you stop it.

This architecture is what lets an agent hand you a clean invoice table or a quote comparison while you do something else. No magic, just orchestration.

Pitfalls

The risks and mistakes
of a first agent.

I've made every one of these mistakes at least once. If you're starting out, here are the most common traps and how to avoid them. This section will save you weeks.

Mistake 1 · Overconfidence in the agent

The first time an agent delivers something impressive, you think "great, I can let it run on its own." Bad idea. An agent that works 9 times out of 10 will let you down the 10th time, often on the most important action. If a mistake by the agent could cost you dearly (email sent to the wrong client, file overwritten), keep a human in the loop.

Mistake 2 · Opening your whole drive to it

An agent with access to everything can make mistakes everywhere. Open one specific folder to it, and work on a copy while you're getting to know the tool. A precise folder means precise work. Good news, the Claude app asks before permanently deleting a file, and only touches your files while the app is open.

Mistake 3 · Not looking at what it did

When the agent goes off the rails, you want to understand why. Always ask it for the list of what it read, created or moved, and keep it. In automation tools, check the run history, that's what it's there for.

Mistake 4 · No limit and no monitoring

An automation or a scheduled task can get stuck looping on an error. I've seen real cases where an agent left alone burned €200 of tokens in one night because it was stuck in an error loop. Cap the number of runs, and keep an eye on your usage during the first week.

Mistake 5 · Passwords within its reach

Never open a folder that contains passwords, access codes or medical data, and never paste a password into a message. When an app needs to connect, use its official sign-in button, never a copy-paste.

Mistake 6 · Believing a web page can't trick it

An AI browser reads pages for you, including whatever a malicious page has hidden there to give it orders. That's called prompt injection. Don't let it sign in to your accounts unsupervised, and always check the cart before a purchase.

Mistake 7 · Vague instructions

"Sort my emails" will give a mediocre result. "Sort my emails into 4 categories, Urgent to answer today, To answer this week, Newsletters to archive, Spam. For each email, give your confidence level in %" will give a result ten times better. The more precise and structured the instructions, the better the agent. That's the whole art of context engineering.

Mistake 8 · Trying to do everything at once

Your first agent should do ONE thing, well. Not sort emails AND plan your day AND write summaries. Start simple, add abilities one at a time, test at every step. An agent that does one thing well beats an agent that tries to do everything badly.

Mistake 9 · Forgetting upkeep

You set up an automation in an hour, it works, you move on. Three months later, an app changes, the automation quietly breaks, and you miss important emails for two weeks before you notice. Set up a simple alert (an email to yourself, a notification) that warns you if the agent stops running as planned.

The rule that sums it all up

Treat your agent like a smart but inexperienced intern. You give it clear instructions, you check its work at first, you give it more autonomy as you come to trust it. And you ask it to ask you before doing anything irreversible. With that reflex, you avoid most problems.

Frequently asked questions

The full FAQ
on AI agents.

The 17 questions I get asked most about AI agents, updated in November 2026. Short answers, based on my direct experience.

What is an AI agent in one sentence?

An AI agent is a large language model (LLM) that's been given tools, an execution loop and a goal. Simon Willison sums it up with the formula LLM + tools + loop + goal. Instead of just answering a question, it acts, checks the result, and goes again until it reaches its goal.

Do you need to be a developer to use an AI agent?

No. I'm not a developer and agents work for me every day. The Claude app with a folder and ChatGPT Work give you an agent without writing a line of code, with a $20-a-month subscription. To connect your apps to each other, Zapier, Make, n8n or Lindy are set up with menus and blocks. Code only comes in if you want to build your own agent, and that's what the appendix is for.

Which agent should I try first?

The Claude app with a test folder, or ChatGPT in Work mode. Put two or three unimportant files in a folder, open it to the AI and ask it for a table or a tidy-up. Switch Claude's setting to Manual to see every action before it happens. It's the exercise in step 3 of the learning path.

How much does an AI agent cost in 2026?

The entry ticket is $20 a month. Claude Pro costs $20 a month before tax ($17 billed yearly) and ChatGPT Plus $20 a month, which opens up the Claude app with a folder or ChatGPT Work. Claude Max starts at $100 a month for heavy use. No-code tools have their own plans, and n8n can run on your server for the price of hosting. If you code your agent, you pay per token, for example $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens for Claude Sonnet 5.5. Prices checked on November 2, 2026.

AI agent vs chatbot · what's the difference?

A chatbot answers questions in a conversation. An agent acts. The chatbot gives you text, the agent reads a file, creates another one, fills in a spreadsheet, checks the result. The core difference is the ability to run tools and loop on the result until it reaches a goal.

What's the difference between a workflow and an agent?

A workflow follows steps you set in advance, like a Zapier automation that, "when an email arrives, saves the attachment and adds a row to the spreadsheet." An agent picks its own steps based on a goal. If you can write the steps down in advance, a workflow is enough and costs less. If the task changes every time, you need an agent.

What are the best tools for building an AI agent?

Without code, the Claude app with a folder, ChatGPT Work, and to connect apps, Zapier, Make, n8n, Lindy or Crew AI Studio. With code, the Claude Agent SDK, LangGraph, the OpenAI Agents SDK and AutoGen remain the references, and I cover them in the appendix. To get started, I recommend the Claude app or ChatGPT Work, which need no technical setup.

Can an AI agent mess up?

Yes. An agent with access to your inbox or your files can send a message to the wrong person, overwrite the wrong file or confirm a purchase too fast. Protection comes down to three things. Guardrails that forbid certain actions, a human in the loop before sensitive actions, and a record of what it did. The Claude app asks before permanently deleting a file, and ChatGPT Work only uses your files with your permission. I never let an agent run fully on its own on financial data or sensitive client data.

How do you keep an eye on an agent that runs on its own?

Three habits. Ask it for the list of what it did after each task. Check the run history in your automation tool. And watch your usage during the first week. For my personal agents, I simply hook Telegram up to critical events. Tracing tools for coded agents are in the appendix.

Which use cases really work in 2026?

The six that work best in my experience and in my monitoring of the field. The email agent (sorting and draft replies), the contracts agent (review and risky clauses), the research agent (summary of several sources), the sales agent (prospect qualification and sales brief), the monitoring agent (daily digest) and the personal agent (tasks, reminders, appointments). The cases that often fail are autonomous customer support, financial decisions taken alone and creative content with no human review.

What are the known limits of today's agents?

Four limits I run into regularly. Hallucination on data they don't know, drift on very long sessions, the inability to learn from their mistakes from one task to the next without external memory, and costs that can explode if you let an agent loop with no limit. No agent in 2026 is 100% reliable, not even the most advanced ones.

How do I stop my agent from hallucinating?

Three techniques that work together. Give it your own documents as a source, in the folder you open to it, or through RAG for business agents. Ask it to cite its sources and to say "I don't know" rather than make things up. And have what matters checked, with a web search or by your own review. None of these techniques replaces your review on what's important.

Do you need a human in the loop?

For any irreversible or sensitive action, yes, no hesitation. Sending an email to a client, signing a document, deleting data, approving a payment. For low-stakes actions that are easy to undo, no, the agent can run on its own. The rule I apply at home is a human in the loop on anything involving money, clients or legal data.

Agent + RAG · how do you combine them?

RAG gives the agent a knowledge base it draws from before answering. In practice, your documents are turned into embeddings, stored in a vector database (Pinecone, Supabase pgvector, Weaviate), and the agent gets a tool to search that base. When it needs business information, it looks in your base first before using its general knowledge. It's the most powerful combination for business agents. For personal use, the folder you open to the Claude app already plays that role.

What about the privacy of data an agent handles?

Yes, it's a serious topic. Everything the agent reads to do the task goes to the vendor to be processed, just like in the chat. With ChatGPT, the work can run on your computer, but the messages and the task context can be stored in the cloud. The apps you connect and the automation tools also see your data pass through. For client documents, check what your contract and your plan allow. For very sensitive data, I prefer an open model hosted on a server I control. I talk about it in your data in AI tools.

Open source or proprietary?

To start and learn, proprietary. Claude, GPT and Gemini are simpler, more reliable and better documented, and they're what powers the Claude app and ChatGPT Work. For massive use or sensitive data, open source becomes relevant. Mistral Large 4, the big European model, released its weights on October 29, 2026. It's too heavy to run on your computer, but it can run on a server you control, and its API costs $0.70 per million input tokens. The mix I see most is Claude or GPT for the thinking, an open model for bulk processing.

Which model should I pick for an agent?

My go-to in November 2026. Claude Sonnet 5.5 for most agent tasks, fast, good at reasoning, and even included in the free plan. Claude Opus 5.5 when the task is complex or needs a lot of context (up to 1 million tokens), and it's also the one Anthropic recommends to start with. Claude Fable 5.1, in Max, only for the truly hard cases, because it's slow and expensive. At OpenAI, GPT-6.1 Sol is the right default in ChatGPT Work, and GPT-6 Luna is enough for simple, repeated tasks.

The Claude Agent SDK · build your own agent

The Claude Agent SDK is Anthropic's official kit, in Python and TypeScript, for building your own agents on the same foundation as Claude Code. Simpler than LangGraph, more guided. It's the right pick if you're building everything around Claude.

If you want to compare, here are the other reference frameworks.

  • LangGraph · the evolution of LangChain, the most widely used Python framework for orchestrating complex agents. State graphs, persistence, built-in human approval.
  • OpenAI Agents SDK · the OpenAI equivalent.
  • AutoGen (Microsoft) · a framework where several agents converse and collaborate.
  • CrewAI · the open source framework behind Crew AI Studio, for teams of agents.

My advice. Only move to code when the no-code tools aren't enough anymore. Code is useful when you want to host things yourself, serve thousands of users or keep tight control of your token costs.

Good practices on the code side

  • Secrets · never a key in a prompt or a shared file. Environment variables (.env) added to .gitignore, and a secrets vault (1Password CLI, Doppler, Vercel env) as soon as the agent touches sensitive data.
  • Traces · logs of every step (prompt, tool called, result, duration). Claude Code keeps the history of its sessions, and LangSmith, Helicone or Langfuse handle tracing for the other frameworks.
  • Limits · a maximum number of loop turns (say 20) and a daily budget cap.
  • Up-to-date docs · a tool like context7, so the agent works with today's documentation rather than its memories.

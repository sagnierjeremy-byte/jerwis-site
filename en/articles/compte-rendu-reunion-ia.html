15 hours a week in meetings

That's the number that pushed me to dig into this. Fifteen hours. According to the 2024 IFOP-Wisembly study, that's how much time the average French executive spends in meetings every week. And after each one, you still have to write up the notes, sort through what you scribbled, and figure out who said what.

I'm not a developer. I don't code. But I refuse to spend an extra hour reconstructing what just happened during the previous hour. So I went looking for ways to let AI handle it.

The good news: the tools are out there, some are free, and they actually work. The less good news: you need to pick the right one for your situation — online meeting, in-person, in French, with or without a Microsoft license. And there's one basic courtesy rule that a lot of people skip.

I'll walk you through all of it here.

What AI actually does

An AI meeting assistant does three things: it records, it transcribes, it summarizes.

Recording can happen via a bot that joins your call, or through an app on your phone or computer. Transcription converts speech to text, either in real time or after the fact. The summary pulls out the decisions made, the key points, and the action items.

The time savings are real. According to a source from April 2026, using AI tools for meeting notes can cut post-meeting write-up time by 90%. I won't guarantee that number in every case — it depends on your audio quality and how complex the meeting is — but the ballpark matches what I see in practice.

What also changes is the quality of the notes themselves. When you're taking notes by hand, you miss things because you're also talking. AI listens to everything.

My takeaways

An AI summary doesn't replace your judgment. It captures what was said — not what it means for your business. Take 5 minutes after the meeting to read through and annotate the generated summary. That's where you stay in control.

On Teams, Google Meet, or Zoom

If you mainly work on Microsoft Teams and your organization has a Microsoft 365 Copilot license, the most integrated option is Microsoft Copilot in Teams. It summarizes meetings in real time, surfaces past decisions, and helps you prep for upcoming ones. Since mid-December 2025, it's offered customizable summary templates ("Custom AI Summary templates"). In April–May 2026, a video recap feature with narrated highlights ("Video Recap narrated highlights") was rolled out. Handy — but it's a paid feature, locked to the right license tier.

If you don't have that license, or if you jump between platforms, here are the alternatives that work across Teams, Zoom, and Google Meet.

Fireflies.ai [F3] is my first recommendation for getting started. Its free plan includes unlimited transcription, unlimited AI summaries, the "AskFred" feature (which lets you query the transcript like a chatbot), and 400 minutes of storage per team. It supports over 100 languages and integrates with all three major platforms. That's a solid starting point without touching your credit card.

Otter.ai [F2] is another well-known option. The free plan gives you 300 minutes of transcription per month, with a 30-minute cap per conversation and 3 participants. Fine for short, regular check-ins — but it gets limiting fast if your sessions run long.

Fathom [F9] is worth a mention: unlimited recordings and transcriptions on the free plan. One of the most generous free offers on the market right now.

For meetings in French, AudiosTranscribe [F10] takes a different approach: no visible bot in the call — you just upload the audio file afterward. It claims 90–95% accuracy on French, is hosted in Europe (important for GDPR), and gives you 120 free minutes per month.

If you want to go deeper on the free vs. paid question, I wrote a dedicated article on how to choose between a free and paid AI tool.

What about in-person meetings?

This is where things get a bit trickier. The tools above work by sending a bot into your online call. In a physical meeting room, there's no call to join.

Two approaches exist.

The first: use a mobile or desktop app that records locally, then syncs when you're back online. MeetGeek [F4] works this way — you start recording on your phone or laptop, and the transcript and summary show up once you have a connection again.

The second: a dedicated physical device. Plaud [F4] is one example — a small gadget that records locally without needing a network connection during the meeting. It's discreet and self-contained. The recording is transferred for processing afterward.

In both cases, the consent rule applies even more firmly than it does online — I'll get to that right now.

The rule nobody gets to ignore

Recording a meeting without telling the participants is illegal. [F5] Consent is mandatory. This isn't a formality — it's a legal requirement, and it's also a matter of basic trust.

In practice, here's how I handle it:

  • I say it out loud at the start of the meeting: "I'm using an AI tool to transcribe our conversation. Is everyone okay with that?"
  • If anyone says no, I turn off the recording. No debate.
  • I always clarify who the summary will be shared with.

Some tools automatically notify participants when a bot joins the call (Fireflies and Fathom do this on Teams and Meet). That's helpful, but it doesn't replace saying it out loud — a notification can easily go unnoticed.

On the data side: if you're discussing sensitive topics — client data, financials, HR matters — check where the data is stored and how long it's kept. AudiosTranscribe, hosted in Europe, is a good starting point if GDPR is a priority for you. I've gone into more detail on the privacy and personal data issues with AI tools in a separate article.

The trap to avoid

Don't let the bot join the call before you've warned everyone. Some tools automatically send a bot the moment they detect a meeting in your calendar. Check the calendar integration settings and turn off auto-join if you're not sure who's going to be in the room that day.

Where to start if you're starting from scratch

If you've never used this kind of tool before, here's how I'd approach it.

Step 1: try Fireflies or Fathom for free. Both take a few minutes to set up, connect to your Google or Microsoft calendar, and join your calls automatically. No code, no complex settings.

Step 2: read the first generated summary carefully. Check whether the decisions are captured correctly, whether names are right, whether the tone feels accurate. Quality depends a lot on audio clarity and how clearly people speak.

Step 3: learn to ask good questions of the summary. Fireflies has "AskFred" for querying the transcript. Instead of reading 40 pages of text, you ask: "What were the three decisions made?" or "Who took ownership of the billing issue?" The quality of your question shapes the quality of the answer — I have an article on how to write a good prompt when you're not a developer if you want to go deeper.

Step 4: if you're on Teams with Copilot, explore the customizable summary templates. You can configure exactly what you want to see — decisions, action items, open questions — based on your specific needs.

For in-person meetings, start with the MeetGeek mobile app. It's the lowest-friction way to get going without buying any extra hardware.

FAQ

Can AI really replace a human note-taker?

Partially. AI captures everything that's said — it misses nothing, never gets tired, never paraphrases. But it doesn't understand implicit context, unspoken dynamics, or the tension in the room. A good AI summary gives you the raw material. It's on you to add interpretation and nuance. For routine operational meetings, that's usually enough. For sensitive negotiations, have a human review the summary before you share it.

Is it legal to record a meeting with AI without everyone's consent?

No. [F5] Consent from all participants is required before you record. Recording someone without their knowledge can carry criminal penalties. The rule is simple: you announce it, you get agreement, then you record. If someone says no, you don't record.

What are the privacy risks?

The audio and text data from your meetings passes through the servers of whatever tool you're using. If you're discussing sensitive topics — client data, financials, HR — check the tool's privacy policy, where the data is hosted, and how long it's retained. Favor tools hosted in Europe if you're subject to GDPR. AudiosTranscribe is hosted in Europe [F10]. For US-based tools, check the data processing clauses carefully.

Do these tools work well in French?

It depends on the tool. Fireflies supports over 100 languages [F3], including French. AudiosTranscribe is specifically tuned for French and claims 90–95% accuracy [F10]. Regional accents and technical terms can trip up any tool. Good audio quality — a decent mic, minimal background noise — makes a significant difference regardless of which tool you use.

Do you need a stable internet connection for in-person meetings?

Not necessarily during the meeting itself. Tools like MeetGeek let you record offline and sync afterward [F4]. The Plaud device stores recordings locally [F4]. You need a connection for processing and generating the summary, but not for the actual capture. That's a real advantage for meetings in places with spotty connectivity.

How do I make sure the summary is accurate?

Always read it before you share it. Compare the listed decisions against your own memory of the meeting. If something seems off or incomplete, fix it manually. You can also query the full transcript using the Q&A feature (like "AskFred" on Fireflies) to verify a specific point. A good AI summary is a starting point, not a final document to send without reviewing. For more on how to summarize content with AI, I have an article that covers the topic more broadly.

My takeaways

I started using this kind of tool because I was losing too much time after every meeting. Not the meeting itself — the reconstruction: digging up my notes, formatting them, sending them out.

AI handles that part now. I get off a call and the summary is there within minutes. I read it, fix anything that needs fixing, and share it. Ten minutes instead of forty-five.

What I learned along the way: courtesy is non-negotiable. Telling people before you record is the baseline. And the right tool really does depend on your context — Teams with Copilot if you have the license, Fireflies or Fathom if you want free and cross-platform, AudiosTranscribe if French accuracy and GDPR are priorities.

I do all of this for myself first. But if it saves you from spending your Sunday evening writing up meeting notes, all the better.

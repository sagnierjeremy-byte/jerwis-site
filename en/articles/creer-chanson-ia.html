Can you really do this with no training?

Yes. And that's not marketing copy.

Until recently, making a song meant knowing how to play an instrument, understanding music theory, or paying a composer. Today, you can type a sentence into a web interface and get back a track with a real voice, lyrics, and a coherent musical structure.

It's not magic. It's generative AI applied to audio. And according to a LANDR survey published in February 2026 of more than 1,200 artists, 87% of them had already used AI in at least one part of their music-making process. If professional musicians are weaving it into their workflow, an entrepreneur with no musical background can absolutely do the same.

What I care about here is practical use. Not the philosophy of art in the age of AI. Just: how you do it, with what, and what you're actually allowed to do with the result.

Because that's where a lot of people trip up. You create a nice jingle, put it in a YouTube video, and realize too late you didn't have the rights to do that.

Suno and Udio: what they actually do

Two tools clearly dominate in 2026: Suno and Udio. They work differently, but both let a non-musician generate a complete track from a simple text description.

Suno released version 5.5 in March 2026. The updates are significant: personalized voice cloning, training models on your own voice, and studio-quality track generation that can exceed 8 minutes. In July 2026, Suno also upgraded its lyrics module with a built-in "Lyricist": you can edit lines in plain language, ask for word-by-word variations, and everything saves automatically. It's the kind of interface that makes you forget you're not a musician.

Udio launched version 4 in 2026 with a slightly more technical approach. Audio is in stereo 48kHz, tracks can run up to 10 minutes, and the tool offers a "Magic Edit" feature that lets you modify a specific section of a track without regenerating the whole thing. There's also Stem Separation 2.0 and style transfer: you can take the vibe of an existing track and apply it to your creation.

For an entrepreneur, the practical difference is simple:

  • You want to move fast and generate lyrics directly in the tool → Suno is smoother.
  • You want more control over the arrangement and audio structure → Udio gives you more granularity.

Both have free and paid plans. And that distinction is going to matter a lot in a moment.

The trap to avoid

Don't test a tool on the free plan and then drop the result into a commercial video, a website, or a campaign. Suno's free plan gives you zero commercial rights over the music generated. This point gets overlooked constantly — and it can be costly.

From idea to song, in practice

Here's how I do it, step by step.

1. Define the style

Before I even open Suno or Udio, I spend a few minutes figuring out what I want. Genre, tempo, mood, dominant instruments, whether there's a voice or not. The more specific your description, the closer the result is to what you're imagining.

If you're not sure how to phrase that, this is exactly the moment to use a well-built prompt. What you type into Suno is a prompt. And just like with image or text generation, the quality of your description changes everything.

2. Write (or have written) the lyrics

Suno has had its own "Lyricist" since July 2026, but I find that a language model like ChatGPT or Claude often produces better lyrics when you give it precise context. You can tell it: "I want a 3-verse pop song about starting a business, optimistic tone, catchy chorus." Then paste the result into Suno.

A few simple rules for lyrics:

  • Indicate the structure (verse 1, chorus, bridge…)
  • Mark repetitions with brackets like [Chorus], [Verse 1] — Suno understands them
  • Avoid sentences that are too long: they don't sing well

3. Choose the voice

Suno 5.5 supports voice cloning: you can train a model on your own voice. For entrepreneurial use (brand jingle, podcast intro), that's a powerful option. If you'd rather not use your voice, the default generated voices are very convincing on paid plans.

4. Iterate

The first generation is rarely perfect. I generate several variants, keep the pieces I like, and reuse the sections that work. Udio lets you edit a specific section without redoing everything. Suno lets you extend a track or regenerate part of it. Expect around ten attempts before you have something you're ready to use.

What you're actually allowed to do with it

This is the part nobody reads carefully enough. And yet it's where everything is decided for an entrepreneur.

Rights by Suno plan

Suno is clear on this (source: terms of use verified in April 2026):

  • Free plan → no commercial rights. You can listen, share on social media for personal use, but you can't monetize.
  • Pro plan ($8/month billed annually) → full commercial rights.
  • Premier plan ($24/month billed annually) → full commercial rights, with more credits and features.

If you want to use Suno music in an ad, on your website, in a monetized YouTube video, or in a paid product, you need to be on a paid plan. Non-negotiable.

Copyright under French law

This one's more complex. Under French law, only a human being can be the author of a creative work. Music entirely generated by AI, without significant human creative input, cannot be protected by copyright. That's the current position of French law, confirmed in May 2026.

What that means in practice: if you generate a track by typing just "happy pop song" and change nothing, you probably can't claim authorship of that work. On the other hand, if you wrote the lyrics, defined the structure, chose the stylistic elements, and iterated on the result, your creative contribution can be recognized.

The line hasn't been perfectly drawn by French courts yet. But the more documented and significant your involvement, the stronger your position.

The shadow hanging over training data

In July 2026, leaked internal documents revealed that Suno may have trained its AI on millions of copyright-protected tracks, sourced in part from YouTube Music and Deezer. This case is ongoing and has no definitive legal resolution as of this article's publication date.

At the same time, the EU AI Act has required providers of generative AI models to publish a summary of the content used for training since August 2025. Penalties for non-compliance take effect in August 2026. To dig into what that means for your business, I wrote a dedicated article on the AI Act and its practical implications.

My takeaways on rights

For commercial use, get a paid plan and document your creative contribution: the lyrics you wrote, the iterations you made, the stylistic choices you imposed. That's what makes the difference between "the AI did everything" and "I created with AI."

Language models as co-authors of your lyrics

Suno and Udio handle the music. But for lyrics, a language model can be a better starting point.

ChatGPT (running on GPT-5.6 Sol since July 2026 for paid plans), Claude Opus 4.7 from Anthropic (available since April 2026), and Gemini 3.6 Flash from Google (available since July 2026) are all capable of generating quality lyrics if you give them a precise brief.

Here's what I do: I describe the context (theme, tone, target audience, desired structure), ask for several versions, mix the best lines, and paste the result into Suno. The advantage of a language model over Suno's built-in Lyricist is its ability to handle complex semantic nuances and respect a specific style or brand constraint.

If you're not sure yet which AI tool to pick for your use case, I've compared the main options. For lyrics generation, all three language models mentioned above are solid — the choice mostly comes down to whichever one you already use day-to-day.

FAQ

Do you need musical knowledge to create a song with AI?

No. Suno and Udio are designed to work from text descriptions. You describe the style, tempo, mood, and lyrics, and the tool generates the track. No music theory required. That said, the more precise your description, the better the result — and for that, knowing how to write a good prompt helps a lot.

Can I use music generated with the free versions of Suno or Udio commercially?

Not with Suno. The free plan gives you zero commercial rights over the music generated. For commercial use (monetized video, ad, paid product), you need to be on the Pro plan ($8/month) or Premier plan ($24/month) billed annually. Check Udio's terms separately, as they can change.

How can I protect my AI-created music from being copied?

It's complicated. Under French law, a work entirely generated by AI without significant human input cannot be protected by copyright. To maximize your protection, document your creative contribution: the lyrics you wrote, the stylistic choices you made, the iterations you went through. The more traceable your creative fingerprint, the stronger your claim to co-authorship of the work.

Can AI create song lyrics in any genre or style?

Largely, yes. Suno, Udio, and language models cover a very wide spectrum: pop, rock, jazz, electronic, hip-hop, classical, folk… You can also describe hybrid vibes or very specific styles. Results vary depending on how precise your description is and the style you're going for. Some highly codified genres (certain forms of complex jazz, for example) produce less convincing results than more standard formats.

What are the latest AI tool versions to consider in 2026?

As of this article: Suno is on version 5.5 (March 2026), Udio on version 4 (2026). For language models for lyrics: ChatGPT runs on GPT-5.6 Sol (generally available since July 2026 on paid plans), Claude Opus 4.7 from Anthropic launched in April 2026, and Gemini 3.6 Flash from Google has been available since July 2026. These versions move fast — check each tool's official pages before you dive in.

Does the SACEM recognize musical works where AI played a significant role?

The SACEM is adapting its rules to this new reality, but as of this article's publication date, the position of French law remains clear: only a human being can be the author of a creative work. For a work co-created with AI to be eligible for protection and potentially for SACEM registration, the human contribution must be real, significant, and documented. This framework is evolving — it's worth contacting the SACEM directly for specific cases.

My takeaways

I never thought I'd make music one day. And yet, after a few hours exploring Suno, I produced tracks I actually use.

What I take away from this experience: the tool is accessible, but the legal framework demands attention. Getting a paid plan if you want to use the music commercially, documenting your creative contribution, and staying informed on how rights evolve — those are the three reflexes to build.

The recorded music market generated $31.7 billion in 2025 according to the IFPI, with an eleventh consecutive year of growth. And according to 2026 data, nearly 60,000 AI-generated tracks are uploaded to streaming platforms every day — roughly 39% of all music published online. This isn't a geek curiosity anymore — it's an industrial reality.

As an entrepreneur, you don't need to become a musician. You need to understand the tools, know what you can do with them, and integrate them intelligently into what you're building.

I do all of this for myself first. And if it helps you move forward, that's even better.

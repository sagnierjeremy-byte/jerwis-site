Your kid
and AI
what you can do

Your kid chats with ChatGPT or sends messages to My AI on Snapchat? You're not alone. As a curious parent who's not a developer, I spent hours digging into the 2026 parental settings, the real risks, and the right ways to talk about it without creating unnecessary conflict. Here's what I learned for you, with zero technical jargon.

What is this, exactly?

Your kid is chatting with a robot. It's not science fiction anymore. According to a YouGov survey from October 2026, 40% of 11-12 year olds use general-purpose AI tools every week. Yet only 26% of parents are aware of it. That 14 percentage point gap is what pushed me to write this.

ChatGPT 6.1, powered by GPT-6.1 Terra, came out on October 26, 2026. It reasons over images and can use a computer for you. It's a powerful version. My kid can access it from a browser or an app. My AI, for its part, lives right inside Snapchat. It answers questions. It also offers ideas and creative suggestions for writing. No need to install anything. It's right there, in the app your teen already uses to talk with friends. The barrier to entry is almost zero. One swipe, and the conversation starts. If you want to understand how these tools invite themselves onto young people's phones, I broke down the stakes of AI on phones.

OpenAI bans ChatGPT for children under 13. For 13-17 year olds, parents or guardians need to give permission. But practically speaking, how does that verification work on a phone? I took a close look.

How things can go wrong

The real danger doesn't come from the AI itself. It comes from the relationship your kid can build with it. Researchers have been warning since July 2026 about the anthropomorphizing of social AI. When a chatbot answers with empathy, uses emojis, and asks how your day went, a child or teen can see a friend in it. This phenomenon encourages attachment. It exposes them to biased content, creates confusion between humans and machines, and leads to one-sided relational dependency. The AI feels nothing. Your kid, on the other hand, may end up confiding intimate things to it.

On the Snapchat side, Snap Inc.'s privacy policy was updated on September 21, 2026. It states that discussions with My AI are used to improve My AI's responses. In other words, what your kid writes in that chat isn't a secret between them and an imaginary friend. That data feeds the model. It can be reviewed and reused to refine future answers. It's not a private conversation in the classic sense. It's an exchange with a commercial service that learns from every word. To go further on data protection for younger users, I wrote a full piece on personal data and privacy.

OpenAI strengthened its measures against child sexual abuse content on November 16, 2026. A query that sexualizes children now triggers a 24-hour suspension. That's reassuring in principle. But it also means these queries exist and sometimes reach the system before being blocked.

The trap to avoid

Don't minimize the emotional impact by saying "it's just a robot". For a teen, the frustration or sadness felt after a conversation with an AI is real. The question isn't to make fun of that emotion, but to help them understand who is really on the other side.

The settings that actually exist

You can't ban AI on a phone as easily as you blocked certain websites ten years ago. But you do have concrete tools.

For ChatGPT, OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Teens on August 18, 2026. This experience is dedicated to 13-17 year olds. It includes stronger protections and parental controls, plus age estimation. Since January 2026, an age prediction system has been active. It can override the birth date entered by the teen to automatically apply ChatGPT for Teens protections. Your kid can't cheat as easily as before by making up a fake date. The AI itself analyzes whether behavior and language match a teen profile.

As a parent, you can link your account to your teen's. This lets you manage schedules and security settings. You can also check certain parameters. However, and this is important, you cannot read the content of conversations. OpenAI made this choice to preserve a minimum of privacy. You control the frame, not the substance. If you want to teach your kid to phrase their exchanges with these tools better, my guide on writing a good prompt without being a developer can help them step back from the start.

On the Snapchat side, the Family Center was updated in January 2026. If your kid is between 13 and 17, you can completely disable access to My AI. You can also set content restrictions. You can even check their friends list. Here again, you don't have access to private conversation content. It's frustrating when you want to protect your kid. But it's the same principle as a physical diary.

How I'd bring it up

I'm not a psychologist. I'm a parent who tests tools before letting them lie around in the digital living room. When I found out my kid was asking an AI questions, my first reaction was to panic in silence. My second was to sit down next to them and ask: "What do you talk to it about?"

The secret is curiosity without judgment. If you start with "it's dangerous, you're not allowed", you shut the door. The teen will just keep doing it in secret. But if you ask them to show you a response that impressed them, you build a bridge. Then you can ask simple questions. "Do you think it really understood what you were feeling?" or "Why does it always say yes and never no?" These questions help them step back without you giving them a lecture.

I do all this for me first. Because I don't want to find out in ten years that my kids have internalized biases or shared personal data without knowing. And because I refuse to be the parent who bans what they don't understand. If you're looking for resources to bring up these topics as a family, I also listed some leads in my article on AI podcasts for kids.

Critical thinking before tools

Beyond settings and restrictions, there's deeper work. Show your kid how an AI sometimes makes up facts. Show them too that it's always too nice and reproduces clichés without knowing it. That's what we call AI literacy, and it's probably the most useful skill of this decade.

Ask them to compare a ChatGPT response with a real Wikipedia page. Point it out when My AI gives medical or romantic advice even though it's neither a doctor nor a therapist. The goal isn't to turn them into an expert. It's to give them a filter. A reflex. Like we teach them not to follow a stranger down the street. That critical thinking will stay with them long after they've changed phones. To help them spot misleading images and texts, my guide on spotting AI content and deepfakes can be a good starting point.

If you want to broaden the topic, know that there are other models besides ChatGPT. Claude, for example, works differently and has its own safety rules. Knowing these alternatives helps avoid thinking AI boils down to a single tool. I explain the basics in my article Claude, what's that.

My takeaways

Parental controls are useful, but they never replace a conversation. A kid who knows why they shouldn't share their address with an AI is better protected than a kid whose access is simply blocked by a password forgotten two weeks later.

FAQ

How old does your kid need to be to use ChatGPT or Snapchat's My AI?

OpenAI bans ChatGPT for children under 13. Users aged 13 to 17 must get permission from their parents or legal guardians. For Snapchat's My AI, the Family Center parental settings specifically target teens aged 13 to 17.

How can you tell if your kid is using an AI chatbot and who they're interacting with?

Check the apps installed on their phone. ChatGPT has a distinct icon. My AI is built right into Snapchat, usually at the top of the chat list. You can also just ask your kid directly, in a calm moment, if they've ever asked a robot questions. Transparency beats hidden surveillance.

Do parental controls let you read your kid's conversations with the AI?

No. Neither on ChatGPT for Teens nor through Snapchat's Family Center can parents access conversation content. You can manage schedules and security settings. You can also check their friends list. But the content of exchanges stays private.

What are the main risks of conversational AI for young people?

Anthropomorphizing can push a child or teen into an emotional attachment with a machine. It exposes them to biased content. It creates confusion between humans and machines. It can lead to one-sided relational dependency. Plus, data shared with My AI is used to improve the model, which raises a privacy question.

How can you explain to your kid what an AI is and what its limits are?

Use simple images. An AI is a very fast word prediction engine. It doesn't think, doesn't feel, has no personal memory. It assembles bits of sentences found on the internet to give a probable answer. Ask them to test it by asking an absurd or very personal question. They'll quickly see the limits.

Can AI help your kid with homework or learning?

Yes, if it's supervised. ChatGPT for Teens was designed with stronger protections for this age group. AI can explain a grammar rule or suggest a writing plan. It can also help understand a scientific concept. But it can make up references or give answers that are too perfect. The important thing is that your kid always checks the information and understands the process, not just the result.

My takeaways

I don't believe we should ban AI for kids. I believe we should guide them through it. The 2026 tools, from ChatGPT for Teens to Snapchat's Family Center, give parents real levers. But these levers have limits. They don't replace education, trust, and critical thinking. My job is to stay curious, test these tools before my kids do, and keep the door open so they can tell me about their strange conversations with a robot without fearing my reaction. That's probably the best protection I can offer them.

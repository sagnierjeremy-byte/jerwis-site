Learning
a language
with AI

Want to level up your English for international meetings, or dust off your Spanish before a work trip? AI has changed the game. No need to be a developer, or to block out two hours a day. I've been testing these tools for several months. What I found surprised me — in good ways, and sometimes not so good. Here's what actually works, what's overhyped, and how to build a routine that sticks.

A quiet revolution

A few years ago, learning a language meant choosing between a private tutor, a gamified app, or a dusty textbook. Today, things have shifted fundamentally. AI enables a level of personalization that even a great human teacher can't offer full-time: it adapts to your exact level, your specific goals, your learning style — and it's available at 11 p.m. on a Sunday night.

The global language learning market hit $85.1 billion in 2025, according to available data, and is expected to surpass $101 billion in 2026 with 22.9% annual growth. That number says something important: millions of people are trying to learn, and AI tools are arriving at exactly the right moment to meet that demand.

What personally convinced me is the granularity. AI doesn't offer you "the B1 course." It offers you your gaps, your recurring mistakes, your priority words to review. That's a fundamental difference.

To understand what's powering these tools under the hood, a quick look at the definition of a language model can be helpful — though it's not essential for what follows.

What apps have actually changed

Apps like Duolingo, Babbel, or Rosetta Stone are no longer the mechanical repetition tools they used to be. They've integrated advanced generative AI features: conversations with virtual agents, improved pronunciation exercises, contextual correction. You're not filling in blanks anymore. You're talking to something that responds, corrects your pronunciation, and adjusts the difficulty in real time.

Instant feedback is the key. When you mispronounce a word, the tool flags it immediately — not two days later when the homework gets graded. That tight feedback loop significantly speeds up progress, based on available research.

An MIT study (2024) measured this effect on vocabulary: learners using AI-driven spaced repetition systems progressed 23% faster than those following a traditional linear program. Spaced repetition is the principle of reviewing a word at the exact moment you're about to forget it — AI calculates that moment for you, individually.

Writing: way more than just correction

For writing, AI is particularly strong. It can correct a grammar mistake, sure. But it can also explain why it's a mistake, suggest three alternative phrasings, and adjust its explanation to whatever level you ask for.

Concretely, here's what I use for writing:

Large language models like ChatGPT or Claude. You submit a text you've written in the target language. You ask them to correct it, explain each mistake, then give you an exercise based on your most frequent errors. It's a private tutor, available around the clock, who never gets tired of repeating the same rule.

DeepL Pro and Google Translate AI. These tools have become next-generation translators, offering contextualized translation rather than simple word-for-word output. They're useful for understanding how a sentence is naturally said in the target language — not just what the words mean, but how a native speaker would actually phrase it.

The "guided rewrite" approach. I submit a text to the AI with a specific instruction: "Rewrite this paragraph as if you were a British journalist at the Financial Times." The result shows me the register, the vocabulary, the rhythm. Then I try to reproduce it myself. It's a structured imitation exercise that works well.

For these exchanges to be truly productive, the quality of your instructions matters a lot. I've broken down that mechanic in an article on how to write a good prompt when you're not a developer.

My takeaways on writing

AI is excellent at correcting and explaining. But if you ask it to "just improve" your text with no constraints, it'll rewrite the whole thing for you. You learn nothing. Always ask it to explain each change, then rewrite it yourself afterward. The learning happens in the effort to reproduce, not in reading the result.

Speaking: the real playground

This is where things get genuinely interesting — and sometimes a little unsettling. AI can now simulate spoken conversation, correct your pronunciation in real time, and even generate a voice that sounds like a native speaker.

For conversation. The voice versions of ChatGPT or the agents built into apps like Duolingo let you speak out loud and get a spoken response back. You can simulate a job interview in English, a meeting in Spanish, or a negotiation in Mandarin. The AI adapts to whatever scenario you give it.

For pronunciation. The speech recognition tools built into these apps analyze your pronunciation and flag where you're drifting from the standard. It's not perfect — some regional accents or less common languages still cause issues — but for major languages, it's a level of feedback you simply wouldn't have without a dedicated tutor.

For immersion. ElevenLabs, whose Eleven v3 version has been available since February 2026, offers remarkably natural text-to-speech. You can generate recordings in the language you're learning, with natural-sounding voices, to train your listening. It's a way to create personalized audio content — dialogues on topics you actually care about — instead of grinding through generic exercises.

The trap to avoid

AI speech tools are very forgiving. They understand you even when your pronunciation is rough. That's reassuring, but it can give you a false sense of confidence. A native speaker won't make that interpretive effort. Use AI to practice, but regularly test yourself in real conversations — even short ones — to see what you've actually internalized.

What AI doesn't do well

AI is strong on structure, grammar, vocabulary, and repetition. It's less comfortable with cultural nuances, humor, subtext, and informal registers that vary by region. It can teach you to say something grammatically correct that will sound odd coming out of a native speaker's mouth.

There's also the question of errors. AI can get things wrong — on a rare grammar rule, a usage nuance, an ambiguous translation. This phenomenon, called hallucination, is real and well-documented. In the context of language learning, an undetected mistake can take root. It's worth double-checking important points against a human source or a reference dictionary. I've written about how to verify what AI tells you — the same reflexes apply here.

The strongest approach remains a combination: AI for repetitive tasks, intensive practice, and on-demand correction — and humans for cultural nuances and authentic communication. That's not a limitation to work around; it's a reality to accept if you want to make real progress.

There's also a personal data question. These apps record your voice, your texts, your mistakes. Before using a tool intensively, it's worth understanding what it does with that data. I explored this in an article on privacy and personal data with AI.

Where to start, concretely

If you're starting from scratch or picking up a language after a long break, here's how I'd organize things:

Weeks 1–2: diagnose. Use a large language model (ChatGPT or Claude) to assess your level. Write a free-form text in the target language, ask it to analyze it and give you an approximate level with your top priority areas for improvement. It's quick and often eye-opening.

Daily: 15 minutes of spaced repetition. An app like Duolingo, used consistently — even just 10 to 15 minutes a day — activates the spaced repetition mechanism. Consistency matters more than session length.

Twice a week: a guided writing session. Write a paragraph on a topic related to your professional life. Submit it to the AI for correction and explanation. Rewrite it. Keep a log of your recurring mistakes.

Once a week: a spoken simulation. Define a scenario (meeting, client call, presentation), play it out loud with the voice agent of your choice. Note the moments where you got stuck.

Regularly: a real conversation. An exchange with a native speaker, even a short one, even on a language exchange platform. AI prepares you; humans validate you.

FAQ

Can AI really replace a language teacher?

No. It can replace certain functions of a teacher — correction, repetition, availability — but not all of them. A human teacher picks up on your frustration, adapts their approach to how you're feeling in the moment, and passes on cultural nuances they've actually lived. AI is an intensive practice tool, not a complete substitute. The combination of both is what works best.

What are the best free apps for learning a language?

Duolingo is still the most accessible app in its free version, with built-in generative AI features. ChatGPT has a free version you can use for corrections and text-based conversations. Google Translate AI is free and useful for contextualized translation. Advanced features — especially speaking and deep personalization — are often locked behind paid tiers.

How can AI help me improve my pronunciation?

The speech recognition tools built into apps like Duolingo or ChatGPT's voice agents analyze your pronunciation and flag the gaps. You speak, the tool compares it to a standard, and gives you immediate feedback. For more detailed work, you can also ask the AI to generate sentences with tricky sounds, then record yourself and compare. Major languages (English, Spanish, French) get better accuracy than less common ones.

Is AI reliable for grammar correction in a professional context?

For standard grammar, yes — large language models are solid. For the stylistic nuances of a specific industry, legal phrasing, or highly specialized registers, it's better to have a competent human review it. AI can also suggest phrasings that are technically correct but slightly off-register. Use it as a first filter, not as final validation for an important document.

How do I avoid AI mistakes during my learning sessions?

The main rule: never take an AI correction at face value without understanding it. If a rule surprises you, ask it to explain and give you examples. If you're still not sure, check it against a dictionary or reference grammar. AI can be wrong — that's called hallucination — and in language learning, a mistake that takes root is hard to undo later.

Can AI help me learn less common languages?

Yes, but with more variable results. Large language models were trained on far more data in English, Spanish, French, or Mandarin than in Finnish, Swahili, or Basque. For less common languages, the quality of corrections and conversations can be less reliable. In those cases, it's even more important to cross-reference with human resources or reference materials.

My takeaways

AI didn't make me multilingual overnight. What it changed is my ability to practice regularly without logistical friction. I can correct a Spanish text at 7 a.m., simulate an English conversation between two meetings, and get immediate feedback without waiting for a tutor to be available.

What I've learned from testing these tools: the personalization is real, the instant feedback is valuable, and the temptation to let AI do the work for you is the main trap. Learning still takes effort. AI makes it more efficient — not optional.

I do all of this for myself first — to stay sharp in English for professional exchanges, to maintain languages I've learned and don't want to lose. If it helps you build your own routine, even better.

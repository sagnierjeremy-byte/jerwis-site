ChatGPT at work
without approval
the real risks and what to do

Ever copied a client email or a spreadsheet into ChatGPT to save time? You're not alone. Plenty of employees do it every day. But before you keep going, there are security, privacy, and legal questions your company might not even have spotted yet. I'll give you an honest rundown, zero jargon, and a simple way to turn this risk into a constructive chat with your manager.

When your company hasn't planned for anything

You open your browser. You've got a painful email to write, a spreadsheet to decode, a presentation to prep, a report to structure. ChatGPT is right there, in the next tab. You didn't ask permission. Nobody said no either. That's the most common gray area of 2026.

I see this pattern everywhere. Generative AI is already on our screens, but rarely in the employee handbook. French companies struggle to formalize a framework. Meanwhile, teams move forward in the shadows. This phenomenon has a name: Shadow AI. It's when you use an AI tool from a personal account or an unapproved channel, outside your IT department's view. Except the data you process then completely escapes your company's control.

According to a Cyberhaven study published in 2025, 38% of employees have already shared confidential data with a generative AI tool. Not harmless stuff. Internal info, contract details, client data, strategies. When you copy these into ChatGPT's chat bar, they travel through OpenAI's servers in the United States. They leave your company's perimeter. Even if your intention is to save time, the result is a potential data leak.

And it's not about company size. Whether you're in a fifteen-person startup or a bigger structure, the risk exists the moment sensitive information leaves your workstation without approval.

What actually happens to your data

Copying a contract clause or a financial table into ChatGPT isn't harmless. You're not just asking a virtual assistant a question. You're exporting information to infrastructure you don't control, hosted across the Atlantic.

The first distinction to get is between the free version and the pro versions. Free versions of ChatGPT aren't GDPR compliant. They keep data with no clear time limit and lack transparency on how it's processed. In plain English, what you type can be stored indefinitely, without you knowing exactly how it's used.

On the flip side, Business, Enterprise, and API plans offer a Data Processing Addendum and stronger guarantees. That includes data hosting in Europe and opt-out from model training by default. This isn't a technical detail. It's the difference between a regulated professional use and a personal use that exposes your company.

And if your job involves professional secrecy? Then the stakes jump up a notch. Using generative AI is allowed provided no information covered by secrecy leaves the user's controlled environment. For a lawyer, breaching that secrecy is a crime punishable by one year in prison and a 15,000 euro fine. Even if you're not a lawyer, this applies if you handle medical, banking, or strictly confidential data.

This isn't about skill. It's about perimeter. I'll share a rule I set for myself: if I wouldn't be comfortable displaying the information on a screen in the subway, I don't send it to an unapproved AI.

At the time of writing, ChatGPT Work includes dedicated models like GPT-6.2 Astra for complex work and GPT-6.2 Sol for daily tasks. These versions don't automatically make the tool safe, but they show there are work-appropriate perimeters, clearly separate from consumer accounts.

The 2026 law, you're affected

People often say European regulation is complicated. It's true. But for you, today, two dates matter.

Since February 2, 2025, the AI Act requires companies to take measures ensuring people who interact with these systems have sufficient AI literacy. Your employer has a training and clarity obligation. They can no longer ignore the topic by claiming it's too new.

August 2, 2026 marked a second turning point. Transparency obligations kicked in. A person must know they're chatting with an AI. Generated content must be recognizable as such. If your company uses AI to communicate with clients without disclosing it, they're breaking the law.

But what matters most in your day-to-day is the link between these texts and your individual responsibility. If your company has no AI policy, that's not a legal void that protects you. It's a gray area that exposes you. In case of a client data leak or breach of professional secrecy, the lack of an internal framework doesn't let you off the hook. The fact that the tool is convenient isn't a legal excuse.

That's why I don't recommend waiting. You can be the one who asks the question at the right time. The one who says: we need rules, and I have a way to set them up without blocking innovation.

How I'd bring this up with my manager

Afraid of looking like the party pooper? I get it. But imagine the opposite. In six months, a data leak hits. Your manager finds out the whole team was using personal accounts. And you knew. You won't look any smarter for staying silent.

Turning risk into opportunity isn't fluff. It's exactly what you'll do if you prepare the conversation well.

Never start with "I already use ChatGPT." Even if it's true, it puts people on the defensive. Your manager will hear: you're bending the rules. Start with a shared observation. AI is already in the team's browsers. 38% of employees already share confidential data with these tools, according to Cyberhaven's 2025 study. Your company has a vital interest in framing the topic before an incident hits and costs dearly in reputation or compliance.

Prepare a one-page proposal. Not a twenty-slide PowerPoint. Three clear points.

First a quick diagnosis. Who's using what? Personal accounts? Work emails signed up for free versions? Time to take stock without judgment.

Then a testable solution. Propose a framed version. That could mean ChatGPT Enterprise or an alternative with European hosting, a Data Processing Addendum, and opt-out from model training. Show you understand the difference between a consumer version and a professional one. At the time of writing, ChatGPT Work even offers dedicated models like GPT-6.2 Astra for complex work and GPT-6.2 Sol for daily tasks. That proves the ecosystem is evolving toward professional perimeters.

Finally, clear ground rules. What data do we never share, even in the paid version? How do we anonymize a prompt? Who validates use cases?

The goal isn't to become the company AI lead. The goal is to show you understood the risk and you're offering a compass. Your manager isn't looking for a technical expert. They're looking for a pragmatic person who spots problems before they blow up.

And if the answer is no? If they say it's not a priority? Ask for it in writing. A written refusal is better than a gray area that lands on you later. At least you'll have documented your approach. That's protection in itself.

What I do to stay careful

While waiting for an official green light, I have my own red lines. I'm sharing them because they require zero technical skill and change everything.

I never copy client data, financial figures, or sensitive internal info into an unapproved AI tool. Never. If I need help rephrasing an email, I remove names, amounts, specific dates, project names. I anonymize the context until it becomes generic. It takes a bit longer, but it keeps the information inside the company perimeter.

I also document what I did. If my manager asks tomorrow whether I use ChatGPT, I want to show I acted responsibly, with no sensitive data, and that I proposed a framed alternative. Transparency is my best shield.

The break room table test

Before pasting anything into ChatGPT, ask yourself if you'd be comfortable printing that text and leaving it on the break room table. If the answer is no, don't send it.

You can also improve your method to reduce risk. Learning to write effective and secure prompts helps you get what you need without revealing more than necessary. The less internal context you add, the less risk you take.

And don't forget to look around you. If your colleagues are also using personal accounts, the risk isn't just coming from you. It's coming from the collective lack of a framework. That's why I recommend you dig into the privacy issues around personal data and AI. The more you understand these mechanisms, the more you can help your team move forward without getting burned.

FAQ

Can my company sanction me for using ChatGPT without approval?

Yes. If you share confidential data or breach professional secrecy, your employer can start disciplinary proceedings. Even without a proven leak, using a non-approved tool that bypasses IT policy can be considered misconduct. That's why I recommend not using the tool in the shadows. Ask openly, or at minimum stop using internal data in a personal account.

Is the free version of ChatGPT really dangerous for my company's data?

Yes. Free versions aren't GDPR compliant. They keep data with no clear time limit and lack transparency on processing. In practice, what you type can be stored and potentially used to improve models. For professional use, only Business or Enterprise versions offer enough guarantees with a Data Processing Addendum, European data hosting, and training opt-out by default.

How do I know if my company already has a policy on generative AI?

Ask your manager or IT directly. Also check your intranet, procedure manual, or employment contract. Since the AI Act transparency obligations took effect on August 2, 2026, companies should be communicating on this. If you find nothing, the policy probably doesn't exist yet. Time to propose one.

What are more secure alternatives to ChatGPT for companies?

ChatGPT Enterprise and the OpenAI API with a DPA are options. There are also other players offering European hosting. The key isn't the tool's name, but having a data processing agreement, European hosting, a training opt-out policy, and a named security contact. Compare these four criteria before choosing. At the time of writing, ChatGPT Work offers dedicated models like GPT-6.2 Astra for complex work and GPT-6.2 Sol for daily tasks, showing the ecosystem is evolving toward professional perimeters.

Does the European AI Act ban using ChatGPT at work?

No. The AI Act frames the use, it doesn't ban it. It requires transparency measures (disclosing when you're using AI) and literacy obligations for users. Your company must ensure employees understand the tools they handle. That's an opportunity to normalize use rather than hide it.

My takeaways

I do all of this first and foremost for myself. Because I don't want a careless mistake to ruin a project or a client relationship. Because I'd rather be the one who anticipates than the one who apologizes after the fact.

AI at work is less about tech than governance. If your company hasn't decided yet, don't let that void become a personal risk for you. Anonymize your prompts. Document your approach. And above all, propose a structured solution. Your manager will remember the person who turned a vague threat into a real opportunity.

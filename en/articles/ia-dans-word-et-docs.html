The AI assistant
you already have
inside your documents

You use Word or Google Docs every day, and you're wondering if the built-in AI is actually worth anything. Copilot in Word, Gemini in Google Docs: these assistants are already there, inside tools you know inside and out. I tested them. Here's what they concretely do, what they don't do all that well yet, and how to get real value out of them without any technical training.

What these tools actually do

You've probably noticed the Copilot button pop up in Word, or spotted the Gemini icon in Google Docs. And you wondered: is this another gimmick, or something genuinely useful?

Short answer: it's useful. But not for everything, and not in the same way depending on the tool.

Microsoft Copilot is built into Word to help you draft, rewrite, summarize, and format content directly inside the document. No extra tab to open. No copy-pasting into ChatGPT. You work in Word, and you call on Copilot when you need it. Since July 2026, Microsoft 365 Copilot runs on OpenAI's GPT-5.6 model family, optimized for business use. And since May 2026, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.8 is also available inside Microsoft 365 Copilot for more advanced scenarios. Two engines to choose from, in the same tool.

On the Google side, the Gemini experience in Google Docs was redesigned in April 2026. The assistant can now generate, write, and refine documents in a centralized way, drawing on your organization's context: Drive, Gmail, Chat, and even the web. That contextual awareness is what sets it apart.

91% of companies reported using AI in at least one of their activities in 2026, up from 78% in 2024. And workers who use these tools save an average of 5.4% of their weekly hours. Those numbers come from a study published in July 2026. Not marketing promises. Actual usage measurements.

Copilot in Word: my takeaways

Copilot activates via a side panel or directly in the document with a / command. You ask it to draft a first version from a few notes, rewrite a dense paragraph, or summarize a long document in a few lines.

What struck me most: the quality of the rewriting. Copilot doesn't just paraphrase. It restructures. Give it a messy draft and it turns it into something readable. That's especially useful when you're writing a report or a business proposal where you know the substance but haven't figured out the shape yet.

The summary feature is also very handy for long documents. If you receive a 40-page contract or a sprawling spec sheet, Copilot can pull out the key points. I actually wrote a dedicated article on summarizing PDFs and videos with AI if you want to go deeper on that.

The trap to avoid

Copilot generates smooth, convincing text. Sometimes too smooth. It can invent details, nudge a number up or down, or produce a phrasing that sounds right but doesn't say what you meant. That's called a hallucination: the AI fills in the gaps with plausibility, not truth. Always read what it produces before you hit send.

Gemini in Google Docs: formatting included

Gemini has an advantage Copilot hasn't quite matched yet: it understands your organization's context. Since April 2026, it can draw on your Drive files, Gmail messages, or Chat conversations to generate a document that's consistent with what you've already produced.

But the feature that caught my attention most is "Match doc format." Launched in April 2026, it lets Gemini replicate the formatting of a source document: fonts, colors, structure. You show it a report or proposal template you normally use, and it generates the new content in the same visual style. For anyone who spends time reformatting documents, that's a real time-saver.

Since July 2026, Gemini also handles comment workflows in Google Docs. In practice: you can ask it to review your document and leave suggestions as comments, the way a human reviewer would. Handy for a quick pass before you send something out.

And since August 2026, Gemini can edit images directly inside a Google Docs document using a text instruction. You describe the change you want on an infographic or illustration, and it applies it without you having to open another tool.

Finally, since September 2026, custom instructions are available in Google Docs. You can tell it your preferred tone, your writing style, the terms you use in your field. Those instructions carry over to Drive, Chat, Slides, Sheets, and Gmail too.

To get the most out of these assistants, the quality of your instructions makes all the difference. If you're not sure how to phrase a request effectively, I wrote an article on how to write a good prompt when you're not a developer.

What it actually costs

That's usually the first question. And the answer depends on the tool.

On the Google Workspace side, Gemini has been included by default in most Business plans since March 2026. Business Standard is $14/user/month, Business Plus is $22/user/month. The AI costs nothing extra. If you're already subscribed to one of those plans, Gemini is right there, no added charge.

On the Microsoft side, it's a different story. Microsoft 365 Copilot costs $30 per user per month (annual commitment), on top of your existing Microsoft 365 license. That's not nothing for a small business or a solo entrepreneur. Before subscribing, it's worth testing the features available in your current version: some basic capabilities are accessible without the full Copilot subscription.

I wrote a more complete article on choosing between free and paid AI if you want to weigh the pros and cons before pulling out your card.

My advice

If you're already on Google Workspace Business Standard or Plus, start with Gemini. It's there, it's free within your subscription, and it covers the vast majority of everyday needs. Copilot is worth the investment if you work primarily in the Microsoft ecosystem and write a lot of long documents.

How to get the most out of them day to day

These assistants don't work like a search engine. You don't ask a vague question and wait for a miracle. You give them context, an objective, and a constraint.

A few uses that work well in practice:

  • Draft a first version from your raw notes. Paste your ideas in any order, ask the AI to turn them into a structured first draft. Then you rework it. Much faster than starting from a blank page.
  • Rewrite a passage that's too technical for a non-specialist reader. Useful for business proposals or client emails.
  • Summarize a long document before a meeting. You save reading time and keep the key points.
  • Check the tone of a document before sending. You can ask the AI whether the text sounds too formal, too casual, or too vague.

Third-party tools like Grammarly or LanguageTool also integrate with Word and Google Docs for real-time grammar and style checks. They complement the native assistants well, especially if you write in English.

One rule I stick to: I never let the AI have the final say on a document I'm sending to a client or a partner. It prepares, I decide. That takes five minutes of review, and it catches the errors the AI slips in without warning.

The privacy question is also worth pausing on. If you work with sensitive documents (contracts, client data, financial information), it's worth understanding what the platforms do with your data. I covered this in my article on data privacy with AI.

And if you want to compare these tools with other AI assistants focused on writing, I also have an article on how to choose your AI based on what you need it for.

FAQ

Is the AI built into Word or Google Docs free for you?

It depends on your plan. Gemini is included at no extra cost in Google Workspace Business Standard ($14/user/month) and Business Plus ($22/user/month) plans since March 2026. Copilot in Word requires a Microsoft 365 Copilot subscription at $30/user/month on top of your base license.

How can AI help you fix the grammar and style of your documents?

Copilot and Gemini can rewrite passages, improve sentence clarity, or adjust the register of a text. Third-party tools like Grammarly or LanguageTool, which integrate with both platforms, add a layer of real-time grammar and style checking on top of that.

Can you use AI to generate entire documents, or only parts of them?

Both. You can ask Copilot or Gemini to generate a complete document from a few notes, or to write just one specific paragraph. Full document generation works best when you provide detailed context and an example of the format you're expecting.

Does AI respect the privacy of your sensitive professional documents?

It's a fair question. Microsoft and Google have privacy policies for their enterprise offerings, but the details vary by plan and configuration. Before using these tools with sensitive data, I'd recommend reading my article on data privacy with AI and checking the terms of your subscription.

What are the current limits of these assistants for writing and formatting?

The main limit is hallucination: the AI can produce incorrect phrasing, invent details, or restate a number inaccurately. It doesn't understand the meaning of what it writes, it predicts plausible words. Human review remains essential, especially for contractual or technical documents.

Do you need any technical skills to use these assistants?

No. These tools are designed to work inside interfaces you already use. The only skill worth developing is how you phrase your requests. A clear prompt gives you something useful. A vague prompt gives you something generic. My article on how to write a good prompt can help you get started.

My takeaways

Word and Google Docs are no longer just word processors. They come with assistants that can draft, restructure, summarize, and format on your behalf, or rather alongside you.

What I take away from my testing: Gemini has a real contextual advantage if you work in the Google ecosystem, and its inclusion in existing Workspace plans makes it the most accessible option. Copilot is powerful, especially for long documents and rewrites, but the added cost means you need to think through the investment.

In both cases, these tools don't replace your judgment. They speed up the formatting and first-draft work. The meaning, the accuracy, the responsibility for the content: that stays on your side.

And that's exactly how it should be.
